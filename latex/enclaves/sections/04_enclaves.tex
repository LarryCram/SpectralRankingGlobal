\section{Enclaves}
\label{sec:enclaves}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Motivation}

Citation-based rankings rest on an implicit assumption: that highly cited work
flows from high-prestige sources and institutions, and that low-prestige
sources produce work that the rest of the field largely ignores.  In mature
disciplines with well-established journal hierarchies this is broadly true, but
it is not universally so.  A substantial minority of highly cited papers appear
in sources that the spectral ranking places below the corpus average.  Such
papers constitute what we call \emph{enclaves}: clusters of high-impact work
embedded in a low-prestige publication environment.

Enclaves are scientifically significant for at least two reasons.  First, they
suggest that the publication venue is, for some research communities, a poor
signal of work quality.  The mismatch between citation impact and venue prestige
may reflect disciplinary norms (some communities routinely publish landmark
work in domain-specific journals that receive few citations from outside the
subfield), linguistic or geographic segmentation (non-anglophone research
communities may prefer national journals that are poorly integrated into the
global citation network), or active field formation (emerging sub-disciplines
that have not yet converged on a set of high-prestige flagship journals).
Second, enclaves are relevant to the design of ranking systems: if a ranking
systematically misclassifies a coherent research community, analyses that
depend on that ranking inherit the error.

\subsection{Highly Cited Works}

The foundation of the enclave analysis is the identification of
\emph{Highly Cited Works} (HCW) — works that receive substantially more
citations from within the retained corpus than is typical for their publication
year and field.

For each work $p$ retained in field $f$ (i.e.\ published in a retained source
in field $f$), we count $n_{\mathrm{intra}}(p, f)$: the number of distinct
citations received by $p$ from any work whose source is retained in any field.
This cross-field definition avoids penalising interdisciplinary work: a paper
in Biochemistry that is heavily cited by Immunology researchers counts those
citations regardless of which field's retained set the citers belong to.  When
a citer work appears in multiple field rankings, its prestige score is taken as
the maximum $v$ across all retained fields, preventing the same citation from
being attributed multiple prestige levels simultaneously.

A work $p$ is classified as HCW in field $f$ if
\[
  n_{\mathrm{intra}}(p, f) \;\geq\; Q_{99}\!\left(n_{\mathrm{intra}}(\cdot,
  f)\;\middle|\;\mathrm{year}(p)\right),
\]
where $Q_{99}$ is the 99th percentile of intra-field citation counts among all
works retained in field $f$ that were published in the same year as $p$.  The
year-specific threshold is essential: a paper published in 2014 has had
substantially longer to accumulate citations than one published in 2023, and a
common threshold across years would mechanically assign HCW status almost
exclusively to older works.  We restrict the HCW analysis to works published
between 2014 and 2023, a ten-year window that is wide enough to capture the
citation lifecycles of most research outputs while falling fully within the
flat works corpus (2000--2025) and the citation window (2016--2025).

Across all 26 fields and the 2020--2024 census window, the procedure identifies
341,695 HCW records (one per (work, field) pair; a work retained in multiple
fields can be HCW in each).  These represent approximately 230,000 distinct
works, covering the most-cited $\approx 1\%$ within each field--year cell.

\subsection{Quadrant classification}

Each HCW can be characterised by two prestige dimensions: the spectral score
$v_s$ of its publishing source, and the mean spectral score of its citers.
The citer prestige is measured as the mean source prestige $v_{\mathrm{citer}}$
of works that cite $p$ within the retained corpus, averaged over all distinct
citers.  These two scores define four quadrants:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{c|cc}
 & \textbf{citer } $v < 1$ & \textbf{citer } $v \geq 1$ \\
\hline
\textbf{source } $v \geq 1$ & \textsc{hcw}$^{+-}$ & \textsc{hcw}$^{++}$ \\
\textbf{source } $v < 1$    & \textsc{hcw}$^{--}$ & \textsc{hcw}$^{-+}$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $v = 1$ threshold is the corpus average: units above it receive more
citation return per unit of output than the average unit in their field.
\textsc{hcw}$^{++}$ papers are the conventional highly cited works: published
in high-prestige venues and cited predominantly by high-prestige sources.
\textsc{hcw}$^{-+}$ papers are the primary enclave type: published below the
average venue but cited by above-average sources — works that the high-prestige
part of the field reads and cites, despite originating from a low-prestige
venue.  \textsc{hcw}$^{--}$ papers are below-average on both dimensions: they
are highly cited within the corpus, but by sources that are themselves
below-average.  These may represent internally self-referential subfields or
low-prestige citation communities.

The fraction of HCW with source prestige $v_s < 1$ varies markedly across
fields: from approximately 7--10\% in Arts and Humanities, Social Sciences,
and Agricultural Sciences, to 30--38\% in Pharmacology, Nursing, and
Veterinary.  The higher fractions in clinical and professional fields reflect
the greater heterogeneity of journal quality in those domains and the
importance of specialist publication channels that are poorly integrated into
the broader citation network.

\subsection{Work-level prestige}

To capture the full prestige context of each HCW, we compute a
\emph{work-level prestige score} $v_p^{\mathrm{work}}$ that combines the
publishing source's prestige with the institutional prestige of the authors.
This is defined in Section~\ref{sec:spectral_ranking} as:
\[
  v_p^{\mathrm{work}} \;=\; \frac{v_s + \bar{v}_u}{2},
\]
where $v_s$ is the source prestige and $\bar{v}_u$ is the unweighted mean
prestige of affiliated institutions (falling back to $v_s$ when no affiliated
institution is retained).  The work-level prestige is computed across all 26
fields for all retained works, yielding 59.8~million (work, field) records.

The work-level prestige provides a more nuanced view than source prestige alone.
A paper published in a below-average journal ($v_s < 1$) may be affiliated with
a highly prestigious institution ($\bar{v}_u \gg 1$), indicating that the
mismatch is at the venue level rather than the institutional level: the research
group is clearly high-calibre but chose (or was forced by journal availability,
language, or disciplinary custom) to publish in a less prestigious outlet.
Conversely, a paper from a high-prestige journal affiliated with below-average
institutions may indicate a one-off contribution from a peripheral group that
happened to meet the quality bar of a top journal.  The joint $(v_s, \bar{v}_u)$
pair therefore carries more diagnostic information than either score alone.

\subsection{NMF topic clustering}

Within each field, the HCW with $v_s < 1$ (the enclave candidates) are
heterogeneous: they may arise from several distinct research communities,
each with its own thematic coherence.  To group enclave candidates into
interpretable clusters, we apply Non-negative Matrix Factorisation (NMF) to
the TF-IDF representation of their titles.

For each field, we form a document--term matrix from the titles of all HCW with
$v_s < 1$, after standard text preprocessing (lowercasing, stop-word removal,
unigram and bigram tokenisation).  TF-IDF weighting down-weights terms that
appear in many titles and up-weights terms that are distinctive to a subset of
works.  NMF then decomposes the matrix into $k$ topics, each represented by a
non-negative linear combination of terms, and assigns each work a loading
vector across the $k$ topics; the dominant topic assignment defines the work's
cluster.

The number of topics $k$ is chosen adaptively: $k = \min(10, \lfloor n /
20\rfloor)$, where $n$ is the number of HCW with $v_s < 1$ in the field,
subject to a minimum of 30 works required to run the decomposition.  This
heuristic yields small $k$ for fields with few enclave candidates (where
over-factorisation would produce spurious clusters) and up to 10 topics for
large fields.  Each resulting cluster is interpreted as a thematic enclave: a
coherent group of highly cited works that share a research theme while
appearing in below-average venues.

\subsection{Citation network analysis}

The final stage of the enclave analysis expands each NMF-defined enclave to
its \emph{1-hop citation network}: the set of seed HCW plus all works that
cite any seed within the retained corpus.  This network provides two kinds of
evidence about the nature of the enclave.

First, the network topology reveals whether the enclave constitutes a
self-contained citation community or is embedded in the broader field.  The
\emph{loop rate} — the fraction of citations in the network that run between
two seed works — measures internal self-citation.  A high loop rate would
suggest an isolated citation ring, potentially inflating $n_{\mathrm{intra}}$
for seeds through mutual citation rather than genuine influence.  A low loop
rate confirms that the seeds' high citation counts reflect attention from the
broader community.

Second, the distribution of citer prestige scores ($v_{\mathrm{citer}}$)
characterises who reads and cites the enclave.  If the citers are predominantly
above-average sources, the enclave is genuinely influential despite its
low-prestige publication venue.  If citers are themselves below-average, the
enclave may reflect an isolated low-prestige community that is highly cited
internally but has limited influence on the rest of the field.

For the two fields examined in detail as connectivity checks, Engineering (field
22) and Mathematics (field 26), the network structure is consistent with genuine
influence rather than citation rings: Engineering enclaves have 2,922 seed
works, 259,000 distinct citers, and a loop rate of 0.5\%; Mathematics enclaves
have 1,016 seeds, 40,000 distinct citers, and a loop rate of 1.4\%.  Internal
citation rates (citations between seeds as a fraction of all intra-network
citations) are 11\% and 25\% respectively.  These figures rule out the
orchestrated-ring interpretation and confirm that enclave seeds are genuinely
embedded in the broader citation flow of their fields.

\subsection{Pipeline summary}

The enclave analysis is implemented as a five-stage pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{E1.}] \emph{HCW detection.}  Identify the top 1\% of retained
  works by intra-field citation count per (field, year); attach source and
  institution prestige scores.
  \item[\textbf{E2.}] \emph{TF-IDF.}  Compute term lift (TF-IDF) for the
  vocabulary of HCW with $v_s < 1$ relative to the full HCW population, to
  identify terms that are over-represented among enclave candidates.
  \item[\textbf{E3.}] \emph{NMF clustering.}  Cluster enclave-candidate titles
  into $k$ thematic groups via NMF; retain cluster assignments and top terms.
  \item[\textbf{E4.}] \emph{Network analysis.}  Build the 1-hop citation
  network per (field, cluster); compute loop rate, component structure, and
  mean network prestige.
  \item[\textbf{E5.}] \emph{Researcher identification.}  Identify the top-3
  most frequent authors within each enclave's 1-hop network from the OpenAlex
  authorship records, together with their $h$-index and total work count.
\end{enumerate}
Each stage feeds into the next; the final outputs are per-field markdown
reports combining the network statistics and researcher profiles for each
named enclave cluster.
