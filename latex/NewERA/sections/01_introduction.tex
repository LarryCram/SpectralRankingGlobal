\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Evaluations of the quality and influence of scholarly work across national university systems can help guide higher education policy, shape institutional status and development, and in some cases determine funding.  With so much at stake, the scholarly community strives to control the evaluative methodologies and outcomes, maintaining that only scholarly experts can make the necessary informed judgements. This sets up the cycle of \textit{peer review}, where researchers undertake their own research and evaluate that of others \citep{woodPeerReviewProcess1997a}. A key part of researchers' professionalism, the cycle applies in national research evaluation, selection of applications for funding, pre-publication review, and in assessor reports for appointments, promotions, and prestigious awards. Tension can arise when peer review meets evaluative bibliometrics: bibliometric indicators may inform, audit or discipline peer review, and reviewers may consult bibliometric resources as part of their review. While peer review can be regarded as \enquote{one of the highest forms of nonsense} \citep[][p. 14]{zimanCollectivizationScienceBernal1983}, it can also be expensive: the UK's 2021 Research Excellence Framework (REF) was fully costed at between \pounds 300M and \pounds 560M \citep{netoREF2021Cost2023}. This paper explores a bibliometric method that addresses some of the principal concerns about indicators, tests it against the outcomes of a completed national exercise, and reports results that bear on the question of bias in peer review. Our aim is to inform the design of the assessment that will replace Excellence in Research for Australia (ERA), an exercise that \citet{baghjanatiComparativeStudyResearch2025} compare with the REF.

Both peer review and bibliometric indicators have attracted extensive critical attention, much of it addressing concerns that are particular to specific evaluation contexts. 

On the peer review side, \cite{leeBiasPeerReview2013} survey the major putative forms of \textit{bias}, relating to authors (prestige, institutional affiliation, gender, nationality, and career age) or to content, notably conservatism towards novel and interdisciplinary work \citep[see also][]{boudreauLookingLookingKnowledge2016, bromhamInterdisciplinaryResearchHas2016}. They note that research on peer review faces difficulty, since there is rarely an independent measure of quality against which bias could be established. Their paper closes by observing that \enquote{the inescapable sociality and partiality of peer evaluation raise questions about whether impartiality can or should be upheld as the ideal for peer review.} Beyond bias, agreement between reviewers is low, so outcomes depend substantially on who is assigned to assess a given output \citep{bornmannScientificPeerReview2011, pierLowAgreementReviewers2018}. The phenomenon of \enquote{cognitive cronyism} may also affect panel choices \citep{travisNewLightOld1991}. Panellists cannot read closely the hundreds of outputs allocated to them, and may fall back on publication venue or reputation as unacknowledged proxies \citep{wilsdonMetricTide2015} so that bibliometric factors enter the process invisibly even when formally excluded. Panels are charged with forcing determinations under process architectures and implementations that make \enquote{epistemic truth on academic quality become[s] situational truth} \citep[][p. 143]{milyaevaLetsAgreeAgree2023}. However, not all forms of bias and committee dynamics are risks in national research evaluation. ERA 2018 was retrospective, low-stakes (with little influence on funding or reputation) and conducted at large scale with over 500,000 works, 2,600 units of evaluation, and 1,000 peer reviewers: the scale and cost may have been accepted by universities as a means to legitimise and replicate academic elites \citep{sayerRankHypocrisiesInsult2014}.

Critiques of bibliometric indicators in national research evaluations rest on different premises. \citet[p.~3]{abramoForcedBattlePeerreview2024} proposes the analytically useful distinction that \enquote{peer review effectively evaluates research quality and scientometrics gauges scholarly impact, notwithstanding their limitations and exceptions.} Accordingly, quality is a property discerned as a work is produced, while impact is a property acknowledged after the work is produced. National research evaluation exercises aim to rate quality among other things. If the exercise accepts impact as one pointer to quality, it entails bibliometric indicators of acknowledgement; if not, the exercise overlooks a multitude of informed expert decisions made when works are referenced by scholars. Of course, the limitations of bibliometric indicators are well documented. Citations measure attention rather than quality, and motives are heterogeneous \citep{bornmannWhatCitationCounts2008, tahamtanWhatCitationCounts2019}. Citation distributions are highly skewed and differ systematically across fields, so indicators require careful normalisation \citep{waltmanSystematicEmpiricalComparison2013}. Journal-level measures such as the impact factor are poor guides to the merit of individual articles \citep{seglen1997why}. Database coverage is uneven, disadvantaging the humanities and social sciences, books and non-English publications \citep{mongeonJournalCoverageWeb2016}. Cumulative advantage inflates the citations of already prominent authors and institutions \citep{mertonMatthewEffectScience1968}, while novel research, whose impact is often delayed, may be penalised \citep{wangBiasNoveltyScience2017}. Indicators are also vulnerable to gaming and goal displacement once they carry funding consequences, as Australia's experience with publication-count drivers showed \citep{butlerExplainingAustraliasIncreased2003, biagioliGamingMetricsMisconduct2020}. Composite measures such as the $h$-index have poor formal properties \citep{waltmanInconsistencyHindex2012}.

Criticism of bibliometric evaluation has stimulated manifestos, declarations and reports on research assessment. DORA \citep{allenPracticalGuideImplementing2025}, the Leiden Manifesto \citep{hicksBibliometricsLeidenManifesto2015}, the CoARA Agreement \citep{coara} and \textit{The Metric Tide} publications \citep{wilsdonMetricTide2015, hill2015literature, HEFCE-II,  curryHarnessingMetricTide2022} position expert judgement as a corrective to indicator misuse. In doing so they often implicitly treat peer review as the impartial standard against which bibliometrics is to be disciplined, and sidestep the questions raised by \citeauthor{leeBiasPeerReview2013}. Although \cite{HEFCE-II} showed that metrics are poor predictors of peer review for recent works, \cite{traagSystematicAnalysisAgreement2019} used the same data to argue that because the REF allocates funding to institutions rather than to individual outputs, agreement should be assessed at the institutional level. They used measures based on absolute and relative differences rather than correlations, and modelled the uncertainty inherent in peer review itself. On that basis they found that in Physics, Clinical Medicine, and Public Health, agreement between metrics and peer review is comparable to the internal agreement of peer review; \cite{pridePeerReviewCitation2018} similarly reported high concordance at the institutional level and others have found similar results \citep{butlerMetricsPeerReview2009a, checchiHaveYouRead2021, traagMetricsPeerReview2025}. Traag and Waltman accepted that low agreement at the level of individual publications argues against using metrics to evaluate single outputs \citep[see also][]{bacciniTheyAgreeBibliometric2016} while noting that once peer review is treated as a noisy measurement rather than a gold standard, the case against metrics for aggregate evaluation becomes considerably weaker. It is nonetheless \textit{The Metric Tide} that broadly shaped subsequent policy choices in the UK \citep{frameworkBuildingSuccessLearning2016} and Australia \citep{sheil2023trusting, maryokaneAustralianUniversitiesAccord2024}.

The tensions between bibliometrics and peer review for institutional evaluations are visible in Australian government policies.  On the one hand, under the \textit{Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021}, the regulator (Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency, TEQSA) is statutorily required to assess whether Australian universities meet threshold requirements for research quality, including having regard to \enquote{the volume of citations, and the quality of the publications in which the research output appeared} \citep[][Sect.~5]{teqsa2021determination}. On the other hand, a review of the ERA administrator (Australian Research Council, ARC) echoed the position of \textit{The Metric Tide}: \enquote{We do not recommend that ERA and EI be replaced by a metrics-based exercise because of the evidence that such metrics can be biased or inherently flawed in the absence of expert review and interpretation} \citep[p.~86]{sheil2023trusting}. While not inconsistent, these positions invite bibliometric methods that are tuned to address weaknesses and aligned to support peer review, where possible.

This paper examines a bibliometric approach to national research evaluation based on the bipartite spectral ranking of \citet{docampodomingoRecirculatingCardinalSpectral2026}. The research system relies on journals and institutions. Journals (more generally, sources) filter works through editorial and peer review, archive the literature, and create metadata for retrieval and exploration. Institutions fund individual researchers, implement training, oversee compliance, establish research organisational units and maintain physical and information infrastructure. Together they play distinct, stabilising roles in the social system of science \citep{tahamtanSocialSystemsCitation2022}. In the model, influential sources are those referenced by works from influential institutions, and influential institutions are those whose works appear in, and are referenced by, influential sources. A one-mode projection \citep{breigerDualityPersonsGroups1974} of the normalised reference matrix yields a stationary ranking probability $\pi$ for each unit and, from it, a size-independent influence $v$ scaled so that the output-weighted mean over a field is one. An institution with $v = 2$ attracts twice the mean influence per unit of output, whatever its size. Size independence, field normalisation by construction, and the replacement of journal-level proxies by a jointly determined ranking address three of the concerns listed above. Others, including uneven database coverage and the gap between attention and quality, it does not address.

A bibliometric indicator proposed for national evaluation should be tested against the evaluation it is meant to support. ERA 2018 is a convenient test: its units of evaluation (UoEs), classification, low-volume threshold and ratings are public, and it assessed some disciplines by citation analysis and others by peer review. We emulate the ERA 2018 UoEs with spectral rankings computed from OpenAlex \citep{priemOpenAlexFullyopenIndex2022} and ask three questions. How many rated UoEs can the method reproduce from open data? How closely does spectral influence agree with the ERA rating, and does agreement differ between citation and peer review disciplines? Where the two disagree, is the disagreement associated with characteristics of institutions rather than of their research?

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:spectral_ranking} sets out the spectral ranking model and its computation. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes ERA 2018, the OpenAlex corpus, and the emulation of ERA UoEs. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports coverage, agreement with ERA ratings, and the association of ratings with institutional characteristics at equal spectral influence, and tests how these depend on counting references from outside each field and on the method of assigning works to fields. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the results, their limitations, and their implications for the bibliometric component of a future national assessment.
