\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Evaluations of the quality and influence of scholarly work across national university systems can help guide higher education policy, shape institutional status and development, and in some cases determine funding.  With so much at stake, the scholarly community strives to control the evaluative methodologies and outcomes, maintaining that only scholarly experts can make the necessary informed judgements. This sets up the cycle of \textit{peer review}, where researchers undertake their own research and evaluate that of others \citep{woodPeerReviewProcess1997a}. A key part of researchers' professionalism, the cycle applies in national research evaluation, selection of applications for funding, pre-publication review, and in assessor reports for appointments, promotions, and prestigious awards. Tension can arise when peer review meets evaluative bibliometrics: bibliometric indicators may inform, audit or discipline peer review, and reviewers may consult bibliometric resources as part of their review. While peer review can be regarded as \enquote{one of the highest forms of nonsense} \citep[][p. 14]{zimanCollectivizationScienceBernal1983}, it can also be expensive: the UK's 2021 Research Excellence Framework (REF) was fully costed at between \pounds 300M and \pounds 560M \cite{netoREF2021Cost2023}. This paper aims to help improve national research evaluation by exploring a novel bibliometric methodology. Our recirculating spectral ranking approach addresses some of the principal concerns about bibliometric indicators, and present results that raise questions about bias in peer review. The goal is to propose reliable and efficient ways to incorporate bibliometric indicators into the planned replacement for the Excellence in Research for Australia (ERA) evaluation. \cite{baghjanatiComparativeStudyResearch2025} compare ERA and REF.

Both peer review and bibliometric indicators have attracted extensive critical attention, much of it addressing concerns that are particular to specific evaluation contexts. 

On the peer review side, \cite{leeBiasPeerReview2013} survey the major putative forms of \textit{bias}, relating to authors (prestige, institutional affiliation, gender, nationality, and career age) or to content, notably conservatism towards novel and interdisciplinary work \citep[see also][]{boudreauLookingLookingKnowledge2016, bromhamInterdisciplinaryResearchHas2016}. They note that research on peer review faces difficulty, since there is rarely an independent measure of quality against which bias could be established. Their paper closes by observing that \enquote{the inescapable sociality and partiality of peer evaluation raise questions about whether impartiality can or should be upheld as the ideal for peer review.} Beyond bias, agreement between reviewers is low, so outcomes depend substantially on who is assigned to assess a given output \citep{bornmannScientificPeerReview2011, pierLowAgreementReviewers2018}. The phenomenon of \enquote{cognitive cronyism} may also affect panel choices \citep{travisNewLightOld1991}. Panellists cannot read closely the hundreds of outputs allocated to them, and may fall back on publication venue or reputation as unacknowledged proxies \citep{wilsdonMetricTide2015} so that bibliometric factors enter the process invisibly even when formally excluded. Panels are charged with forcing determinations under process architectures and implementations that make \enquote{epistemic truth on academic quality become()s) situational truth} \citep[][p. 143]{milyaevaLetsAgreeAgree2023}. However, not all forms of bias and committee dynamics are risks in national research evaluation. ERA 2018 was retrospective, low-stakes (with little influence on funding or reputation) and conducted at large scale with over 500,000 works, 2,600 units of evaluation, and 1,000 peer reviewers: the scale and cost may have been accepted by universities as a means to legitimise and replicate academic elites \citep{ sayerRankHypocrisiesInsult2014}.  

Critiques of bibliometric indicators rest on different premises. \cite[][p. 3]{abramoForcedBattlePeerreview2024} proposes the analytically useful distinction that \enquote{peer review effectively evaluates research quality and scientometrics gauges scholarly impact, notwithstanding their limitations and exceptions.} Quality is a property discerned as a work is produced, while impact is a property acknowledged by others. To the extent that a national quality evaluation exercise accepts impact as one pointer to quality, it entails bibliometric indicators that indicate the degree of acknowledgement. The limitations of those indicators are equally well documented. Citations measure attention rather than quality, and the motives for citing are heterogeneous \citep{bornmann2008citation}. Citation distributions are highly skewed and differ systematically across fields, so indicators require careful normalisation, and journal-level measures such as the impact factor are poor guides to the merit of individual articles \citep{seglen1997why}. Database coverage is uneven, disadvantaging the humanities and social sciences, books and non-English publications \citep{mongeon2016journal}. Cumulative advantage inflates the citations of already prominent authors and institutions \citep{merton1968matthew}, while novel research, whose impact is often delayed, may be penalised \citep{wang2017bias}. Indicators are also vulnerable to gaming and goal displacement once they carry funding consequences, as Australia's own experience with publication-count funding showed \citep{butler2003explaining, biagioli2020gaming}. Composite measures such as the $h$-index have poor formal properties \citep{waltman2012inconsistency}.

The manifestos, declarations and reports on research assessment respond almost entirely to the second of these literatures. DORA \citep{allenPracticalGuideImplementing2025}, the Leiden Manifesto \citep{hicksBibliometricsLeidenManifesto2015}, the CoARA Agreement \citep{coara} and \textit{The Metric Tide} publications \citep{wilsdonMetricTide2015, hill2015literature, curryHarnessingMetricTide2022} all position expert judgement as the corrective to indicator misuse. In doing so they implicitly treat peer review as the impartial standard against which bibliometrics is to be disciplined, and sidestep the questions raised by \citeauthor{leeBiasPeerReview2013}. \textit{The Metric Tide} has been both the most influential of these documents and the most sceptical. Its correlation analysis of REF2014 found that individual metrics produced significantly different results from peer review, and concluded that metrics could not provide a like-for-like replacement for it \citep{hefce2015correlation}. That conclusion, however, takes peer review as the benchmark of validity. Reanalysing the same data, \cite{traag2019systematic} argued that because the REF allocates funding to institutions rather than to individual outputs, agreement should be assessed at the institutional level. They used measures based on absolute and relative differences rather than correlations, and modelled the uncertainty inherent in peer review itself. On that basis they found that in Physics, Clinical Medicine, and Public Health, agreement between metrics and peer review is comparable to the internal agreement of peer review; \cite{pride2018peer} similarly reported high concordance at the institutional level. Traag and Waltman nonetheless accepted that low agreement at the level of individual publications argues against using metrics to evaluate single outputs \citep[see also][]{baccini2016do}. Once peer review is treated as a noisy measurement rather than a gold standard, the case against metrics for aggregate evaluation becomes considerably weaker. It is nonetheless \textit{The Metric Tide}'s position that has shaped subsequent policy, including in Australia.

In Australia, there is tension between the criteria applied by the Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA) when registering or renewing an institution as an Australian university and the justification used to discontinue the Excellence of Research for Australia (ERA) evaluation. Under the \textit{Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021}, TEQSA is statutorily required to assess whether Australian universities meet threshold requirements for research quality. Specifically TEQSA has regard to \enquote{the volume of citations, and the quality of the publications in which the research output appeared} \citep{teqsa2021determination}. On the other hand, co-temporaneous reviews of the ERA administrator, Australian Research Council, \citep[\textit{Trusting Australia's Ability};][]{sheil2023trusting} and the Australian Universities Accord \citep{maryokaneAustralianUniversitiesAccord2024} recommended discontinuation of ERA and its companion Engagement and Impact (EI) assessment. The ARC review echoed the position of \textit{The Metric Tide}: \enquote{We do not recommend that ERA and EI be replaced by a metrics-based exercise because of the evidence that such metrics can be biased or inherently flawed in the absence of expert review and interpretation} \citep[p.~86]{sheil2023trusting}.

This paper proposes an evidence-based framework for national research evaluation using a \textbf{bipartite spectral ranking model} \citep{docampodomingoRecirculatingCardinalSpectral2026}. Rather than evaluating journals or institutions in isolation, we model scientific influence as a mutual reinforcement network between two distinct entity classes: publishing channels (sources, $\mathcal{S}$) and research institutions ($\mathcal{I}$). High-prestige sources are those cited by leading research institutions, and high-prestige institutions are those that publish in, and are cited by, influential sources. 

By applying a one-mode Breiger projection \citep{breigerDualityPersonsGroups1974} and computing the dominant Perron--Frobenius eigenvector, we derive an intrinsic, size-independent prestige intensity metric, $v$. This metric is calibrated so that the global baseline across any discipline is exactly unity ($v = 1.0$). An institution with $v_I = 2.0$ produces research attracting twice the global expected prestige per unit of published output, whether the university publishes 100 or 10,000 papers. By decoupling intellectual influence from gross publication volume, the spectral model addresses TEQSA's requirement for evaluating publication quality and citation influence while eliminating the size distortions identified by the ARC review.

To connect this global model to national policy, we construct an audited concordance between the open scholarly graph provided by OpenAlex \citep{priemOpenAlexFullyopenIndex2022} and the Australian and New Zealand Standard Research Classification \citep[ANZSRC 2020;][]{anzsrc2020} Fields of Research (FOR). Moving away from proprietary, paywalled databases in alignment with the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information, the framework organizes evaluation across two tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A macro-level \textbf{5 Broad Research Areas (AREA5)} framework that preserves Mathematics and Computing Sciences (MCS) as an autonomous analytical pillar (reflecting conference-based dissemination and avoiding dilution within engineering), while properly aligning Veterinary Science with Agricultural, Food and Earth Sciences (LES) rather than human medicine.
    \item A meso-level \textbf{26-discipline field tier}, mapping directly to the two-digit ANZSRC FOR 2020 divisions.
\end{itemize}

Evaluating all 42 Australian Higher Education Providers (HEPs) across the 2020--2024 census window reveals distinct institutional profiles across university mission groups. While the Group of Eight (Go8) maintains broad-based intensity ($\bar{v}_I \in [1.71, 2.01]$), specialized technological institutions in the Australian Technology Network (ATN) achieve premier status in computing ($v_I = 3.21$ at Swinburne and $2.80$ at UTS), outperforming comprehensive institutions. Institutions with focused missions, such as Australian Catholic University (ACU) in social sciences ($v_I = 2.92$), and regional universities in the Regional Universities Network (RUN), demonstrate targeted competitive strengths that aggregate volume metrics obscure. Parameter and geopolitical bloc sensitivity analyses confirm that these spectral rankings remain stable ($\rho_s > 0.98$) under OECD and G20 restrictions.

The paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we formulate the bipartite network of sources and institutions, derive the one-mode Breiger projections, and describe the numerical methods used to compute prestige intensities. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the OpenAlex corpus, the filtering and retention thresholds, and the concordance between OpenAlex topics and the ANZSRC 2020 classification. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical results across the 42 Australian higher education providers, compares institutional profiles with the United Kingdom and United States, and tests the robustness of the rankings against parameter and geopolitical variations. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications of these findings for national research evaluation and regulatory assurance, and outlines directions for future work.
