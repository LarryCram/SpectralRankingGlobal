\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

National research evaluation systems assess the quality and influence of scholarly work across universities. In the United Kingdom, the progression of national assessment through the Research Assessment Exercise (RAE) and the Research Excellence Framework (REF) prompted an inquiry into whether quantitative indicators could replace or support labor-intensive peer panels. The resulting independent review, \textit{The Metric Tide} \citep{wilsdon2015metric}, alongside its accompanying literature survey \citep{hill2015literature}, mapped international assessment systems up to 2015 and examined the technical and behavioral weaknesses of metrics. 

That inquiry identified several structural defects in conventional bibliometric indicators:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Size and Volume Bias}: Raw publication counts and aggregate citation tallies systematically favor large, comprehensive universities over smaller, specialized, or regional institutions \citep{docampo2014internal, docampo2015effect, docampo2017academic}. Size-dependent measures reward institutional scale rather than research intensity or quality per output.
    \item \textbf{Journal Impact Factor (JIF) Fallacy}: Ascribing quality to an individual work based on the journal in which it appears ignores the extreme skewness of citation distributions within journals \citep{seglen1997why, hicks2015leiden}. Journal-level proxies fail to reflect work-level reception or the stature of citing institutions.
    \item \textbf{Field Incommensurability}: Publication frequency, citation densities, and co-authorship norms differ by orders of magnitude across disciplines, rendering unnormalized comparisons across fields statistically invalid.
    \item \textbf{Gaming and Perverse Incentives}: Naive citation indicators are susceptible to strategic manipulation, including citation cartels, self-citation clubs, and paper mills, conforming to Goodhart's law whenever quantitative targets drive funding or prestige.
\end{enumerate}
From a critical perspective, \textit{The Metric Tide} highlighted these metric pathologies in part to defend the traditional authority of peer review, codifying ``Responsible Metrics'' as normative aspirations---robustness, humility, transparency, diversity, and reflexivity---rather than resolving the underlying mathematical limitations of citation analysis.

The Australian experience post-dates \textit{The Metric Tide} by several years. The Excellence in Research for Australia (ERA) framework, established in 2010, conducted five national evaluations through 2018 using retrospective institutional submissions evaluated by peer review and citation benchmarks \citep{arc2019era}. While ERA mapped national research activity, it incurred substantial compliance costs. Following the independent review of the Australian Research Council Act \citep[\textit{Trusting Australia's Ability};][]{sheil2023trusting} and the Australian Universities Accord \citep{accord2024final}, the Australian Government announced the formal discontinuation of ERA and its companion Engagement and Impact (EI) assessment. The ARC review explicitly echoed the cautionary conclusions of \textit{The Metric Tide}:
\begin{quote}
``We do not recommend that ERA and EI be replaced by a metrics-based exercise because of the evidence that such metrics can be biased or inherently flawed in the absence of expert review and interpretation'' \citep[p.~86]{sheil2023trusting}.
\end{quote}

Discontinuing ERA, however, created an unresolved regulatory problem. Under the \textit{Higher Education Standards Framework (Threshold Standards) 2021}, the Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA) is statutorily required to assess whether registered higher education providers meet threshold requirements for research quality. In its formal \textit{Quality of Research Determination 2021}, TEQSA specified that it must have regard to ``the volume of citations, and the quality of the publications in which the research output appeared'' \citep{teqsa2021determination}. Australian higher education governance thus became caught in an apparent contradiction: the regulator is mandated to evaluate publication quality and citation volume, yet the national evaluation framework was retired because conventional metrics are demonstrably biased, distorted by scale, and prone to gaming without costly panel oversight. It remains unclear how regulators and institutions can satisfy this statutory requirement without reverting to either burdensome submissions or flawed raw citation counts.

This paper proposes an evidence-based framework for national research evaluation using a \textbf{bipartite spectral ranking model} \citep{docampo2025screening}. Rather than evaluating journals or institutions in isolation, we model scientific influence as a mutual reinforcement network between two distinct entity classes: publishing channels (sources, $\mathcal{S}$) and research institutions ($\mathcal{I}$). High-prestige sources are those cited by leading research institutions, and high-prestige institutions are those that publish in, and are cited by, influential sources. 

By applying a one-mode Breiger projection \citep{breiger1974duality} and computing the dominant Perron--Frobenius eigenvector, we derive an intrinsic, size-independent prestige intensity metric, $v$. This metric is calibrated so that the global baseline across any discipline is exactly unity ($v = 1.0$). An institution with $v_I = 2.0$ produces research attracting twice the global expected prestige per unit of published output, whether the university publishes 100 or 10,000 papers. By decoupling intellectual influence from gross publication volume, the spectral model addresses TEQSA's requirement for evaluating publication quality and citation influence while eliminating the size distortions identified by the ARC review.

To connect this global model to national policy, we construct an audited concordance between the open scholarly graph provided by OpenAlex \citep{priem2022openalex} and the Australian and New Zealand Standard Research Classification \citep[ANZSRC 2020;][]{anzsrc2020} Fields of Research (FOR). Moving away from proprietary, paywalled databases in alignment with the Barcelona Declaration on Open Research Information, the framework organizes evaluation across two tiers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A macro-level \textbf{5 Broad Research Areas (AREA5)} framework that preserves Mathematics and Computing Sciences (MCS) as an autonomous analytical pillar (reflecting conference-based dissemination and avoiding dilution within engineering), while properly aligning Veterinary Science with Agricultural, Food and Earth Sciences (LES) rather than human medicine.
    \item A meso-level \textbf{26-discipline field tier}, mapping directly to the two-digit ANZSRC FOR 2020 divisions.
\end{itemize}

Evaluating all 42 Australian Higher Education Providers (HEPs) across the 2020--2024 census window reveals distinct institutional profiles across university mission groups. While the Group of Eight (Go8) maintains broad-based intensity ($\bar{v}_I \in [1.71, 2.01]$), specialized technological institutions in the Australian Technology Network (ATN) achieve premier status in computing ($v_I = 3.21$ at Swinburne and $2.80$ at UTS), outperforming comprehensive institutions. Institutions with focused missions, such as Australian Catholic University (ACU) in social sciences ($v_I = 2.92$), and regional universities in the Regional Universities Network (RUN), demonstrate targeted competitive strengths that aggregate volume metrics obscure. Parameter and geopolitical bloc sensitivity analyses confirm that these spectral rankings remain stable ($\rho_s > 0.98$) under OECD and G20 restrictions.

The paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we formulate the bipartite network of sources and institutions, derive the one-mode Breiger projections, and describe the numerical methods used to compute prestige intensities. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the OpenAlex corpus, the filtering and retention thresholds, and the concordance between OpenAlex topics and the ANZSRC 2020 classification. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the empirical results across the 42 Australian higher education providers, compares institutional profiles with the United Kingdom and United States, and tests the robustness of the rankings against parameter and geopolitical variations. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications of these findings for national research evaluation and regulatory assurance, and outlines directions for future work.
