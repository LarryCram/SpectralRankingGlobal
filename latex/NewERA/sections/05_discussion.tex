\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Where the reference network was dense enough to support a ranking, spectral influence computed from open data reproduced the ordering of ERA 2018 ratings within divisions to a moderate degree, with pooled rank correlations of about 0.5. The agreement was as strong in disciplines that ERA assessed by peer review as in those it assessed by citation analysis. The second observation matters more than the first. In citation disciplines the committees saw citation indicators, so some agreement with any citation-based measure is expected. In peer review disciplines the committees worked from reviewers' reports on sampled outputs, and the spectral ranking, built from references to all of an institution's indexed works, is an independent measurement. That two such different procedures agree to this degree suggests that each captures some of the same underlying property, although neither can be taken as the standard for the other.

These correlations are of similar magnitude to those reported in comparisons of metrics and peer review at the institutional level. \citet{traagSystematicAnalysisAgreement2019} found that, for several units of assessment in REF 2014, agreement between metrics and peer review was comparable to the agreement between peer reviewers, and \citet{pridePeerReviewCitation2018} reported similar concordance at the institutional level. \citet{bacciniTheyAgreeBibliometric2016}, by contrast, questioned the agreement claimed for the Italian research assessment. Our design differs from these studies in two respects: the indicator is a ranking of institutions computed jointly with a ranking of sources, rather than an aggregate of article-level citation counts, and the comparison is with the outcome of a completed exercise rather than with a sample of reviewers' scores. We have no measure of the internal reliability of ERA ratings, so we cannot say whether a correlation of 0.5 approaches the ceiling set by the noise in the ratings themselves.

The spectral ranking and ERA disagree about the level of Australian research. Half of the matched UoEs in citation disciplines, and two thirds in peer review disciplines, had influence below the world mean, yet most of these were rated at or above world standard. The two scales differ in construction. The world mean of $v$ is weighted by output over all institutions retained in a field, and every institution's credit is shared among co-authoring institutions. ERA's benchmarks and the committees' notion of world standard were defined differently, and in peer review disciplines world standard was not defined numerically at all. The discrepancy is therefore not by itself evidence that ERA ratings were inflated. It does show that a statement such as \enquote{above world standard} depends on how the world is counted, and a successor exercise that adopts bibliometric benchmarks will need to state that choice explicitly.

At equal spectral influence, UoEs in Go8 universities received higher ratings, by about a third of a point in citation disciplines and about one point in peer review disciplines. The association was robust to excluding the divisions where the ranking is unreliable. Two explanations are open, and our data cannot separate them. The first is that ERA committees and reviewers gave weight to institutional reputation. This is the affiliation bias surveyed by \citet{leeBiasPeerReview2013}, and it is consistent with the observation of \citet{wilsdonMetricTide2015} that panellists who cannot read every output fall back on venue and reputation. Its larger size in peer review disciplines, where committees had more latitude, fits this reading. The second explanation is that $v$ misses aspects of performance in which Go8 units are strong. ERA committees also considered research income, applied measures such as patents and commercialisation income, books and non-traditional outputs, and these are not captured by references among works indexed in OpenAlex. A test between the two would need the indicators that ERA committees actually saw, which are not public.

The question of committee representation can be answered more briefly. A UoE whose institution had a member on the rating committee received a rating 0.19 points higher at equal $v$ (s.e. 0.08). Go8 universities held 53 of the 150 committee places, however, and once Go8 membership is included the representation coefficient falls to 0.10 (s.e. 0.08) and is not distinguishable from zero. ERA's conflict-of-interest rules excluded members from rating their own institutions. We find no evidence of a representation effect beyond the Go8 association, although with 506 UoEs the analysis could not detect an effect of that size.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several features of the emulation bear on the results, and for each we note the likely direction of its effect. First, the matched UoEs exclude most of law, the creative arts, history and philosophy, and they over-represent UoEs with higher ratings. The narrower range of ratings would be expected to lower the correlations rather than raise them, but the results say nothing about the disciplines in which ERA relied most heavily on peer review of books and non-traditional outputs. Second, ERA UoEs were defined by institutions' own assignment of outputs to FoR codes, often with strategic intent, whereas the emulation assigns works to codes through a many-to-one crosswalk from OpenAlex subfields. Misassignment adds noise to $v$ and would attenuate agreement, most severely at the four-digit level, which may explain the weaker agreement for citation disciplines at that level. Third, errors in OpenAlex affiliations and institution disambiguation also add noise, and they are likely to be larger for smaller institutions. Fourth, the corpus changes between OpenAlex snapshots. For the same UoEs, two snapshots three months apart (which also differed in the inclusion of conference papers) gave within-division rank correlations of $v$ of 0.90 at the two-digit level and 0.83 at the four-digit level. Finally, the regressions are associational. Go8 membership stands for a bundle of institutional characteristics, and the coefficient should not be read as the effect of any one of them.

\subsection{Implications for national assessment}

Three implications follow for a bibliometric component of the assessment that will replace ERA. First, an open and size-independent ranking reproduces a substantial part of the ordering produced by a large peer review exercise, at negligible marginal cost, across most of the science, social science and professional disciplines. Second, the method is not usable where the reference network is sparse. That limit can be read from the network itself, through the number of retained units and the spectral gap, before any ranking is published, and in those fields assessment must rely on other evidence. Third, a ranking that is blind to institutional identity provides a reference against which peer review outcomes can be audited. A systematic difference between ratings and the reference, such as the Go8 association found here, is not proof of bias, but it identifies where an assessment's judgements depend on something other than the reception of the work and should be explained. This is a role for bibliometrics that supports peer review rather than replacing it, consistent with the position taken in the review of the ARC \citep{sheil2023trusting}, and one that it can perform transparently.

\section{Conclusion}

We emulated the units of evaluation of ERA 2018 with a bipartite spectral ranking of sources and institutions computed from OpenAlex, and compared spectral influence with the published ratings. Three quarters of rated two-digit units could be emulated, with the losses concentrated in law and the humanities. Within divisions, influence and rating were moderately correlated, and equally so in citation and peer review disciplines. At equal influence, units in Group of Eight universities were rated higher, especially in peer review disciplines, while representation on the rating committee added little once Go8 membership was taken into account. Further work should extend the comparison to ERA 2015 and to the four-digit level with a finer crosswalk, add the sentinel treatment of references from outside each field, and test whether the indicators that ERA committees saw account for the institutional premium.
